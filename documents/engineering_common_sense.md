# Engineering Common Sense — Maintenance & Workshop Knowledge

## Purpose

This document contains practical engineering judgment and common workshop practices that can support maintenance planning, spare-part selection, repairs, and work-order recommendations.

These are general engineering practices. Final decisions must consider equipment drawings, manufacturer specifications, material requirements, tolerances, safety procedures, and qualified engineering judgment.

---

## 1. Fastener and Bolt Selection

### Bolt Length

If a required bolt is **M20 × 300 mm**, but the available bolt is **M20 × 400 mm**, the longer bolt may be used **if the application allows it**.

Where appropriate:

* Take the M20 × 400 mm bolt to the workshop.
* Cut it to the required approximate length.
* Deburr and clean the cut end.
* Restore the thread if necessary using the appropriate die or thread-cleaning method.
* Ensure sufficient thread engagement remains after installation.
* Confirm that the modified bolt still meets the required strength and specification.

**Important:** Do not modify a high-strength, safety-critical, or specially manufactured fastener without confirming that the modification is acceptable.

### General Rule

A slightly longer standard component may sometimes be adapted to replace a shorter component when:

* Material/specification is equivalent.
* Diameter and thread type are correct.
* Required strength is maintained.
* The modification does not compromise safety.
* Final dimensions are suitable for the application.

---

## 2. Nuts, Washers and Fasteners

When replacing a bolt, consider the complete fastening arrangement:

* Bolt diameter
* Thread type
* Bolt grade/class
* Bolt length
* Nut compatibility
* Washer requirements
* Required thread engagement
* Installation torque

Do not select a replacement based only on diameter.

For example, an M20 bolt of an unsuitable strength grade should not automatically replace the specified M20 high-strength bolt.

---

## 3. Shafts and Mechanical Components

When a shaft, pin, spacer, or similar component is unavailable in the exact required length, consider whether:

* A longer standard component can safely be machined.
* A shorter component can be replaced with another approved configuration.
* The component can be manufactured locally.
* The required material grade is available.
* The final dimensions and tolerances can be achieved.

Machining should be performed according to the required drawing, dimensions, tolerances, surface finish, and material requirements.

---

## 4. Bearings

When replacing a bearing:

* Confirm the bearing number.
* Confirm bore diameter.
* Confirm outside diameter.
* Confirm width.
* Confirm load requirements.
* Confirm speed requirements.
* Confirm sealing arrangement.
* Check whether the bearing is suitable for the operating environment.

A bearing with the same physical dimensions is not automatically a suitable replacement.

---

## 5. Belts, Chains and Similar Components

For conveyor belts, chains, and other continuous components:

* Confirm dimensions.
* Confirm material/specification.
* Check operating load.
* Check environmental conditions.
* Check compatibility with existing components.

For a damaged section, a temporary repair may sometimes be possible, but the repair must not be treated as a permanent solution where it could create a safety or reliability risk.

---

## 6. Workshop Repair vs Replacement

Before replacing a component, consider whether it can be safely repaired or refurbished.

Possible workshop activities include:

* Cutting
* Drilling
* Grinding
* Welding
* Machining
* Thread repair
* Surface preparation
* Fabrication
* Bushing replacement
* Shaft repair

However, repair should only be recommended when the repaired component can meet the required mechanical strength, dimensions, tolerances, and safety requirements.

---

## 7. Substitution of Spare Parts

If the exact spare part is unavailable, do not automatically recommend the closest-looking item.

Check:

1. Function
2. Dimensions
3. Material
4. Strength
5. Operating conditions
6. Manufacturer/specification
7. Compatibility with connected components
8. Safety implications

If these cannot be verified, recommend confirming with engineering or the equipment manufacturer.

---

## 8. Maintenance Planning Common Sense

When planning a maintenance job, consider more than the failed component.

Ask:

* What caused the failure?
* What parts are required?
* What consumables are required?
* What tools are required?
* What manpower is required?
* Is lifting equipment required?
* Does the equipment need isolation?
* Is there an electrical or mechanical hazard?
* Will the job affect production?
* Is testing required after the repair?
* Are additional components likely to be damaged?
* Should related components be inspected while the equipment is already stopped?

---

## 9. Opportunity Maintenance

If equipment is already stopped for a planned maintenance activity, inspect related components where practical.

For example:

If a conveyor is stopped to replace an idler:

* Inspect nearby idlers.
* Check belt alignment.
* Inspect the belt condition.
* Check the supporting structure.
* Inspect nearby bearings and fasteners.

This can reduce repeated equipment downtime and identify developing problems early.

---

## 10. Temporary vs Permanent Repairs

Always distinguish between:

### Temporary Repair

Used to restore operation while waiting for the correct repair or spare.

### Permanent Repair

Restores the equipment to the required engineering specification.

A temporary repair should be documented and followed by a plan for permanent correction when required.

---

## 11. Practical Cost Consideration

When the exact spare is unavailable, compare:

**Option A — Purchase exact replacement**

versus

**Option B — Safely modify/fabricate an available component**

Consider:

* Material cost
* Workshop cost
* Labour
* Time
* Downtime
* Safety
* Reliability
* Engineering approval
* Expected service life

The cheapest option is not necessarily the best maintenance option.

---

## 12. Safety-Critical Principle

Engineering common sense must never override:

* Lockout/Tagout procedures
* Manufacturer requirements
* Engineering drawings
* Equipment ratings
* Applicable standards
* Safety procedures
* Approved maintenance procedures

When there is uncertainty, the recommendation should be to verify the requirement with a qualified engineer or the equipment manufacturer.

---

## 13. Example Decision

**Requirement:** M20 × 300 mm bolt.

**Available:** M20 × 400 mm bolt.

**Reasoning:**

The available bolt has the correct diameter but is longer than required.

If the bolt grade, thread type, material, and application are suitable, the longer bolt may potentially be taken to the workshop and cut to the required length. The cut end should be properly finished and the final installation should maintain adequate thread engagement and clearance.

---

Leak Identification by Visual Water Response: When several similar bolted connections are suspected of leaking and the leak source is difficult to identify, a controlled water application may sometimes help identify the source. Apply a small amount of water around the suspected connections and observe the response. A connection that causes abnormal splashing, bubbling, movement, or immediate displacement of the water may indicate the location of escaping fluid or gas. This method should only be used where water application is safe and compatible with the equipment and process conditions. For pressurized, hazardous, hot, or electrical systems, use the approved leak-detection method instead.

**Recommended action:**

> Verify bolt grade and application → approve modification → cut to required length → deburr/thread-finish → install using the specified nut/washer and torque → inspect after installation.

This is an example of practical engineering judgment, not a universal rule.
